xg-from-words code

A football data experiment

Football's best data costs money. Its commentary is free.

The numbers behind “expected goals” are built from cameras that track every player. Almost nobody can afford them. But every match also has someone writing down what happened, in ordinary English, for free.

So: how much of the expensive thing can you rebuild from the words?

The answer, first

0%

of what a camera-based model can tell apart, recovered from one English sentence per shot.

Measured on 8,825 shots where both sources describe the same moment. Everything below is how that number was arrived at — including the part where it nearly wasn't.

Question one

Can commentary tell you a goal is coming?

Pundits say it constantly: they've got all the momentum now. If that is real, the words should show it before the score does.

Four seasons, 22,147 moments, each asking: does either side score in the next fifteen minutes? A model that guesses randomly scores 0.50. A perfect one scores 1.00.

It cannot

Even the model that sees the future only reaches 0.60.

That is the useful finding. The words are not failing to read a signal that is there — the signal is barely there at all. Which side scores in the next quarter of an hour is mostly down to whether a shot goes in, and nothing before it says much about that.

But something turned up

The words describing shots are far richer than the rest.

Fouls and corners are written from a template — the same handful of sentences, over and over. Shots are not. They say where it was struck from, with which foot, and how the chance was made.

Which is exactly what an expected-goals model is made of.

Read straight off the sentence

First, a trap

The sentence tells you the answer.

Every shot's commentary opens by saying what happened. Feed a model the whole line and it does not learn football — it learns to read the first two words.

A model given the raw text scores 1.0000. Flawless, and worthless.

Removing that took four attempts. Two of them looked clean and were not — the second was only caught by printing what the model had learned and finding the word “goal” weighted above everything else. The fix that worked keeps only phrases known to describe the chance, and a test now fails the build if the outcome ever creeps back in.

Question two

How good was that chance?

A different question, and an answerable one. Not when will a goal come — but was the shot just taken a good one?

To check the answer, both sources had to describe the same shots. There is exactly one season where that is possible: 2015-16, the last Premier League season with camera data published openly. The model was trained on recent football and tested there — a decade outside anything it had seen.

Words against cameras, same shots

The gap is real, and small. What the words genuinely cannot say is distance in metres, the angle, and how many defenders were in the way — and that is all the gap turns out to be.

Does it travel?

Trained on English football. Never told about anywhere else.

It does slightly better abroad than at home.

Which is the whole argument in one chart. Camera data exists for a handful of competitions. Commentary exists for hundreds — and it reads the same everywhere, so one model covers all of them.

What it looks like during a match

The scoreline is not always the game.

Goalless at the hour, with one side having created two and a half times as much. Then two goals from half a chance, the other way.

It does not predict the goal. It tells you who deserved one.

What was deliberately left out

Six tools were proposed. All six were measured, and declined.

ToolWhy it was proposedWhat the measurement said
SparkToo much data for one machine The whole corpus is 4 MB. It loads in 0.04 seconds.
An LLMA better reader would close the gap Every model given the whole sentence did worse than seventeen fields pulled out of it.
vLLMServe explanations at scale Needs a kind of graphics card this machine does not have.
C++Python is too slow live 2.4 ms per match, against a 15-second budget — and 60× cheaper than waiting for the network.
KubeflowWeekly retraining A model ten years stale loses 0.009. There is nothing to retrain.
LangGraphOrchestrate the steps Four steps, one branch. That is an if.

Each has a script in the repository that reruns the measurement in seconds. Adding a tool that earns nothing is not a neutral act — it is a claim you cannot defend.

Still open

One question has no evidence behind it yet.

Everything about reading a match live assumes the words arrive quickly enough to be worth reading. No match has been running at a moment when that could be checked. A job now runs during Premier League kickoffs to measure it, and the answer will appear in the repository rather than here.
